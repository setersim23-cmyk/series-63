
The Map of Authorities
Every law, rule, and case behind the Series 63 — what each one is, and where it sits
Appendix to the Coded Textbook · August 2, 2026
Start with a correction to my own advice
In the Master Supplement I told you not to invest effort in the section numbers, on the reasoning that the numbering in your notes looked unreliable. I checked that against NASAA’s own published outline of the Act, and I was wrong. Your citations are almost entirely correct — and the reason they looked odd to me is itself the single most useful thing in this appendix.
Why §401(c1) and §401(c2) exist, and why they confused me
There are two versions of the Uniform Securities Act of 1956 in circulation. The original 1956 text has one set of subsection letters. NASAA later amended that text — adding investment adviser representatives, federal covered advisers, and federal covered securities — and rather than renumbering the whole definitions section, it inserted the new definitions as §401(c1) and §401(c2), immediately after §401(c), broker-dealer. That is why the lettering looks like it has a bolt-on: it does. The NASAA-amended version is the one the exam is written from, and it is the version your notes are citing. Trust them.

One consequence worth internalizing. If you look up a section number in your own state’s blue-sky statute, or in a law review article, or in the 2002 revision of the Act, the numbers will not match. Michigan’s version puts “agent” at §401(c). The 2002 Act renumbers everything into the 100s and 500s. None of that is what NASAA tests. Stay inside the NASAA-amended 1956 numbering and ignore everything else.
That said, the practical advice underneath the wrong reasoning still holds: NASAA writes almost no questions that turn on a section number. Learn the map so the architecture makes sense, not so you can recite citations.
The six layers
Every authority on this exam sits in one of six layers, and knowing which layer something belongs to answers a surprising number of questions on its own — because the layers have different reach, different enforcers, and different relationships to each other.

The whole web, arranged by source of law. Read top to bottom: Congress makes statutes, the SEC makes rules under them, NSMIA draws the preemption line, the states enact their own statute below it, NASAA supplies model rules the states adopt, and the SROs and courts fill in from the side.
FEDERAL STATUTE — acts of Congress. Four matter here, plus the Investor Protection Act. They create the SEC, define the federal perimeter, and set the outer limit of what a state may do.
SEC RULE — rules the Commission adopts under those statutes. Cited by number, not by section: 10b-5, 15c3-1, 206(4)-2. A rule can never exceed the statute it was written under, which is why every SEC rule traces back to a parent act.
THE NSMIA LINE — not a layer so much as a horizontal cut through the whole structure. It is the single most consequential thing in this diagram and it is the reason state law looks the way it does.
STATE STATUTE — the Uniform Securities Act as each state enacted it. Four parts: conduct, persons, securities, general provisions. This is the body of law the exam is actually about.
NASAA — model rules, statements of policy, and model acts. NASAA is a voluntary association of state regulators, not a government body. It has no power of its own; its products bind only where a state adopts them. The exam treats them as though they are law everywhere.
SRO AND CASE LAW — FINRA and MSRB rules, which bind their members by contract rather than by statute, and the handful of court decisions that supply tests the statutes assume.

The layer question that answers questions
When a stem asks who can do something — impose a fine, bar a person, issue an injunction, require registration — identify the layer first. A state Administrator operates under the state statute and can do everything Part IV grants and nothing else. FINRA operates under a membership contract and can expel or suspend a member but cannot reach a non-member. Only a court can enjoin, imprison, or grant immunity. Half the Remedies questions on this exam are layer questions in disguise.

Wrong or unverifiable — fix these six
In your map
The correction
“Administrator’s office — USA §406, §413”
§406 is right. §413 is administrative files and opinions — the public register and interpretive opinions, not the office. The rulemaking power you probably wanted is §412, which also carries the good-faith reliance defense.
“Notice filing — USA §307”
I could not verify a §307 in the NASAA-amended text; Part III appears to run 301 through 306. More importantly, this conflates two different notice filings. The FEDERAL COVERED ADVISER notice filing is §202(b) — that one is certain. The federal covered SECURITY notice filing sits in Part III. Cite §202(b) for advisers and leave the securities one uncited.
“NASAA 102(f)-1 — agency cross”
NASAA’s §102 runs (a) through (e); there is no (f) in the outline. The reliable authority for agency cross conditions is Advisers Act §206(3) and SEC Rule 206(3)-2. The state hook is the unethical practices rule, 102(a)(4)-1.
“Written findings — §204(f)”
§204 is right; the sub-letter is not confirmable and varies between printings. Cite §204 and stop there.
“Rule 506 … but NOT Rule 504 or Rule 505”
Rule 505 was repealed in October 2016. Say “Rule 506 only — not Rule 504.”
“Publicly-quoted secondary market with 3+ market makers”
Not a §402(b) exemption. What the Act actually provides is the manual exemption — a non-issuer transaction in a security whose issuer information appears in a recognized securities manual.

Missing — nineteen authorities your map does not reach
Grouped by how much they matter. The first group is genuinely load-bearing; the third is completeness.
LOAD-BEARING. §101 — the antifraud provision itself, which your map refers to as “the golden rule” without ever citing. §102(a) through (e) — the entire adviser-conduct spine: unethical conduct, solicitation, contracts, compensation, custody. §203(a) through (e) — the entire post-registration chapter: books, information furnished, financial reports, corrections, inspection power. §405 — the statutory home of “registration is not approval,” which is a whole exam cell with no citation behind it in your notes.
STRUCTURAL. §201(a), (c), (d) — the registration requirement broken out by person type, with the de minimis exemption spanning §§201(c), 201(d), 401(f) and 401(g). §202(b) through (f) — adviser notice filing, fees, successor firms, minimum capital, surety bonds. §301 — the securities registration requirement. §305 — the one-year effective period, escrow and impound. §403 — the state advertising-filing rule. §404 — misleading filings. §412 — rulemaking and the good-faith reliance defense. §415 — the statutory policy of coordinating with federal law.
FEDERAL. Securities Act §5 and §17(a). Exchange Act §10(b) as the parent of Rule 10b-5, and §28(e) as the soft dollar safe harbor. Advisers Act §202(a)(11), §205 and §206. The Investment Company Act of 1940. SIPA 1970. Dodd-Frank 2010. SEC Rules 205-3, 206(3)-2 and 203A-1.
NASAA AND OTHER. All six NASAA model rules by number. The Information Security and Privacy Rule. FINRA Rule 2121 and Regulatory Notice 10-22. The Uniform Prudent Investor Act.

What the gaps have in common
Look at the load-bearing group again: §101, §102, §203, §405. Those are the conduct and post-registration provisions — the parts of the Act that govern what a registered person must DO, as opposed to how they GET registered. Your map is a very good map of entry requirements and enforcement, and a thin one of the obligations in between. That is the same shape as the content gap I found in the $2 Textbook, which makes sense: the map was built from it. The coded textbook fills the substance; this appendix supplies the citations underneath.
